I did my master's in the College of Engineering at Carnegie Mellon in the 2020s, and being Indonesian there was a privilege. In my program, Indian and Chinese students made up at least 80% of the class. It takes no expert to see it; just read the names on the graduation list. Each group has its own crowd. I wasn't capped at either, so I could move between both. It helped that I speak some Chinese.

Both groups work very hard. They just show it differently, and it makes more sense once you look at where each one is coming from.

## Indian students

Most of my Indian classmates came on a one-way ticket, figuratively and for some literally. Many took out loans to pay for the degree, so they treated it as an investment that had to pay off. That meant good grades, hard and visible courses, research under a well-known professor for the career connections, and going all in on job fairs. It wasn't unusual to see classmates skip class or work around their coursework to prepare for and attend interviews. It's a full package, and for some of them it's a business decision that will change their lives.

## Chinese students

My Chinese classmates had a similar drive, but a degree from the US also holds its value back home, so they had more options and, I think, felt a bit less pressured. They had a bit more fun, and some were very well off. They tended to live in the nicer apartment complexes, the kind with a doorman and a gym, and then you'd walk into the apartment and find it barely furnished. Maybe they were just lazy. The funny part is that for some of them, the US was the backup plan: they didn't get into their preferred Chinese university, so they went abroad instead.

In engineering they tended to be more reserved. They could write very good code or produce excellent technical work and still be shy about presenting it or speaking up in class. They also grouped together more visibly. An all-Chinese project team was easy to spot. All-Indian teams existed too; they were just less obvious.

## Language

Both groups often spoke their own language among themselves, and that never bothered me. Whenever I joined them, people would switch to English, or proactively translate for me, especially on the Chinese side. Both sides also appreciated it when I tried to speak their language, even when I botched it completely. Hindi was much harder for me. I only got as far as *chalo* ("let's go") and a few curse words.

The best perk was going to a Chinese restaurant with them. I could just sit back without saying anything while they ordered all the good dishes, and none of it was orange chicken. The same goes for Indian food: no chicken tikka masala.

## The ecosystems

I can't blame either group for sticking together. With that many students, each has its own ecosystem for almost everything.

Take meal vouchers. Undergrads are required to buy a meal plan, and most can barely use it up. They start selling the leftover meals at the beginning of the year at half price, so a $12 meal goes for $6, and by the end of the semester it's down to about $2. If you don't know, you don't know.

Or take TA positions, which mostly aren't posted on any portal. The head TA is usually one of the professor's PhD students, and the professor often lets them recruit the rest of the team. Like anywhere else, they pick people who were referred to them or whom they already know. You still have to be good at the job. Success comes from merit plus being ready when the opportunity shows up, and the ecosystem decides who hears about the opportunity first.

The same goes for everything outside class: when to start job hunting, and which courses to take. Course choice is very strategic in a master's program. You might take only three courses a semester, so you don't want to get stuck with a professor everyone else avoids, and the course website won't tell you who that is. The crowd will. Some professors and labs are better known in one crowd than the other, so each side's advice covered a different part of the department. Being in between, I got both halves of the map.

## In between

Being in between meant I got to see both worlds and take the good parts of each. One of my better decisions came from watching what everyone else did with the last fall and spring breaks before graduation. Nobody treated them as a vacation. They're fake weeks off that people spend preparing for the final stretch of classes and the job search, so I did the same.

I also got both sets of holidays. I saw Chinese New Year done properly, which let me tell my mom I was still celebrating it from far away. I also went to Holi and the other big Indian celebrations. Along the way I picked up enough to tease my Indian friends who were no longer quite as vegetarian as they used to be back home.

You could argue the downside is that you never fully belong. I don't think that was true for me. My classmates came from cities all over their own countries, and some of them carried their own regional prejudices with them. As an outsider, I started from a neutral, clean slate.

I'm still close with a handful of them. I've received wedding invitations, I have a couch to crash on whenever I'm in Columbus, and whenever we catch up there are new stories.
